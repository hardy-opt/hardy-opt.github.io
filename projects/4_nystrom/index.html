Regularized Nyström curvature for large-scale optimization

Newton's method costs O(nd² + d³) per step. Sampling a few columns of the Hessian and reconstructing it with the Nyström method brings that to O(ndm + dm²), and a regularizer keeps the step usable when the Hessian is indefinite.

NysReg-Gradient: regularized Nyström curvature

The short version. Second-order optimization methods converge quickly because they use curvature, and almost nobody uses them at scale because curvature is expensive. This work samples a small number of Hessian columns, reconstructs an approximation of the whole matrix from them, and adds a regularizer that keeps the resulting step usable even on non-convex problems. The cost per iteration falls from \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\) to \(O(ndm + dm^2)\), with \(m\) around 5 to 20 percent of the feature dimension.

My contribution. This is my own line of work, developed during my PhD at Kyoto University and continued since. I designed the method, proved the convergence results, implemented it, and ran the experiments. Co-authors on the papers are listed on the Publications page.

Problem statement and motivation

Large-scale optimization sits between two unsatisfying options.

First-order methods such as gradient descent are cheap. One pass over the data gives the gradient, at \(O(nd)\) for \(n\) samples and \(d\) parameters. They converge slowly, and the rate degrades with the condition number of the problem: on a long narrow valley, gradient descent zig-zags across it instead of travelling along it.

Newton’s method uses the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, which tells you how the slope is changing and therefore how far to step in each direction. Near a solution it converges quadratically, and the condition number stops mattering because you are rescaling by the geometry itself. The price is \(O(nd^2)\) to form the Hessian and \(O(d^3)\) to invert it.

That \(d^3\) is the obstacle. At \(d = 10^3\) it is a billion operations. At \(d = 10^4\) it is \(10^{12}\), and ten thousand parameters is a small model.

Performance of the NGD variants on the adult and ijcnn1 datasets: training cost and test error against CPU time, which is the axis that matters when the question is whether curvature is affordable.

Core methodology

The Nyström approximation

Rather than compute the Hessian and then compress it, sample it. Choose \(m \ll d\) column indices at random. Computing only those columns gives a thin matrix \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}\), and the corresponding rows and columns give a small intersection matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\).

The Nyström method reconstructs an approximation of the full matrix from that slice:

\[N_k \;=\; C \, M_k^{+} \, C^{\top}\]

where \(M_k^{+}\) is the pseudo-inverse of the best rank-\(k\) approximation of \(M\), obtained from its singular value decomposition, with \(k \le m\). The full \(d \times d\) Hessian is never formed at any point.

The construction is classical: it comes from numerical integration and is the same machinery behind low-rank kernel approximation. What is new here is applying it to the Hessian inside an optimizer, and making that survive contact with a non-convex objective.

The regularized update

The update takes the form

\[w_{t+1} \;=\; w_t \;-\; \eta_t \, \big(N_t + \rho_t I\big)^{-1} \nabla f(w_t)\]

and the regularization parameter is adaptive, scaled by the current gradient norm:

\[\rho_t \;=\; c_1 \, \|\nabla f(w_t)\|^{\gamma}, \qquad c_1 > 0\]

Three variants were studied, differing only in the exponent:

Variant \(\gamma\) Behaviour
NGD \(1/2\) mildest regularization, closest to pure Nyström-Newton
NGD1 \(1\) regularization decays in proportion to the gradient
NGD2 \(2\) strongest early damping, most conservative

Why the regularizer is the contribution, not the sketch. On a non-convex objective the true Hessian is indefinite: it has negative eigenvalues, because the iterate is near a saddle rather than a bowl. A direction built from an indefinite matrix is not guaranteed to point downhill at all, and a low-rank approximation inherits the problem.

Adding \(\rho_t I\) makes \((N_t + \rho_t I)\) positive definite, so the step is a descent direction by construction. Tying \(\rho_t\) to the gradient norm means the damping is strong when far from a solution and relaxes as the gradient shrinks, recovering fast local behaviour. That single change is what makes the method applicable beyond convex losses.

Avoiding the inverse

Inverting a \(d \times d\) matrix would undo the saving. The Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity avoids it: because \(N_t\) has rank at most \(k\), write \(N_t = Z_t Z_t^{\top}\) and then

\[\big(N_t + \rho_t I\big)^{-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{\rho_t} I \;-\; \frac{1}{\rho_t^{2}} \, Z_t \Big(I_k + \tfrac{1}{\rho_t} Z_t^{\top} Z_t\Big)^{-1} Z_t^{\top}\]

Everything on the right is either a scalar multiple of the identity or an operation on a \(k \times k\) matrix. The per-iteration cost becomes \(O(dm)\) with \(m \ll d\), which is what makes the method practical.

Stochastic variant

For large datasets, NSGD uses mini-batch stochastic gradients every iteration while refreshing the Nyström approximation far less often, typically once per epoch. The regularization parameter for an epoch is computed from the stochastic gradient norm of the previous one. Curvature changes more slowly than the gradient estimate, so refreshing it at the gradient’s frequency would be wasted work.

Experimental results

How large does the sketch need to be?

Effect of sketch size $$m$$ on NGD1 across datasets: training cost, iterations, test error and gradient norm. The sketch size is the one real hyperparameter of the method, and this is the study that tells you how to set it.

Small sketches suffice. On a dense dataset such as gisette, a sketch of 5 percent of the dimension is competitive. Sparse datasets such as w8a need more, around 15 to 20 percent, because the Hessian information is spread more thinly across coordinates and fewer sampled columns capture it.

This is the practical takeaway: \(m\) is problem-dependent and the dense/sparse distinction is a usable rule of thumb, but the method does not choose \(m\) for you.

Theoretical contributions

Global convergence is established under standard assumptions, with an analysis of the global complexity, and the method is shown to achieve superlinear convergence under appropriate conditions. The analysis bounds the difference between the NGD direction and the true Newton direction, which is what connects the approximation quality to the convergence rate.

There is also a clarifying result about how this relates to Newton Sketch. The Nyström approximation can be written as

\[X^{\top} U_k U_k^{\top} X + \rho I\]

where \(U_k\) is an orthogonal matrix derived from the structure of the data itself. Newton Sketch uses a random embedding matrix in the same position. The comparison suggests why Nyström can be more accurate at small sketch sizes: it is sampling along directions the data actually occupies, rather than projecting onto random ones.

Application: brain tumour classification from MRI

The method was applied to fine-tuning a pre-trained network for brain tumour detection, a setting chosen because it is data-scarce, which is exactly where getting more out of each gradient matters.

Limitations

  • Small \(d\): use Newton. The sketch avoids a cost you are not paying.
  • Very noisy gradients or tiny batches. Curvature estimated from noise is worse than no curvature, and a well-tuned first-order method wins.
  • Well-conditioned problems. Gradient descent is already close to optimal and the extra machinery earns nothing.
  • \(m\) must be chosen, and the method is sensitive to it. The study above informs the choice; it does not remove it.
  • Transformer-scale models are untested. The experiments are convex problems and small networks. I would not claim the result extends to large-scale pretraining without running it.
  • The MRI result rests on 76 test images. Treat it as a demonstration that the optimizer is usable in a data-scarce fine-tuning setting, not as a medical imaging result.

Significance

The useful part of this work is not that a sketch is cheaper, which is arithmetic. It is that a regularized sketch gives a usable descent direction on a non-convex objective, which is the property that decides whether a second-order method can be used on anything other than a convex loss. The connection to Newton Sketch also clarifies why data-derived sampling directions can beat random ones at small sketch sizes.

The same construction carries into the federated setting, where the binding constraint is a client device’s memory rather than a datacentre’s compute. That is Nys-Fed.
