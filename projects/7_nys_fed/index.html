Second-order federated learning on edge devices

Newton-type methods converge in far fewer communication rounds than FedAvg, and nobody uses them because a Hessian for a 25M-parameter model does not fit on a client. A sampled low-rank sketch changes that.

Nys-Fed: making curvature affordable on a client device

My contribution. This is the federated chapter of the Nyström curvature line I developed during my PhD. I contributed the regularized Nyström construction the method is built on, the convergence argument, and the design of the federated algorithm. Published at IEEE IJCNN 2026 (WCCI) with Sagnik Ghosh, Arnav Bhavsar, Varun Dutt and Dinesh Singh, from work with the group at IIT Mandi. Implementation by Sagnik Ghosh. Why federated learning is a communication problem

In federated learning the data never leaves the client. Each device computes something from its own data and sends only that to a parameter server, which aggregates and returns a global model. Nothing privacy-revealing crosses the network.

The cost that dominates is communication rounds. Every round means each participating device uploading, the server aggregating, and the result coming back down, across links that are slow, metered and unreliable, to devices that may not be awake. So the question is not “how many gradient steps?” but how few rounds can we finish in?

FedAvg and FedProx are first-order methods. They are cheap per round and take many rounds, and they degrade when client data is non-IID, which in practice it always is.

The second-order case, and why it is normally ignored

Newton-type methods use curvature rather than slope alone, so they point more directly at the minimum and need far fewer rounds. On non-IID data the advantage is larger still, because curvature carries local geometry that an averaged gradient discards.

Newton-type descent against the SGD-based paths used by FedAvg, FedProx and FOMO. Fewer, better-directed steps, if you can afford the curvature.

The reason nobody does it is arithmetic. For \(d\) parameters the Hessian is \(d \times d\). For ResNet-18, \(d \approx 11.7\) million, so the Hessian holds about \(1.4 \times 10^{14}\) entries: over half a petabyte in 32-bit floats. You cannot form it, store it, invert it, or send it.

Existing second-order federated methods work around this in limited ways. FedNew computes the Hessian once and refreshes periodically, but a refresh costs as much as plain Newton. FedNS and FLeNS use Newton sketching with a random transform, which reduces what is communicated but still needs the Hessian to produce the sketch. DONE approximates the local Hessian with Richardson iterations. All improve the direction; none makes the memory problem disappear on a device with a few hundred megabytes of GPU.

The method

Do not approximate the Hessian after computing it. Sample it.

Pick \(m \ll d\) column indices at random. Computing only those columns gives a thin matrix \(\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}\), with \(\mathbf{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) the intersection block. The Nyström method reconstructs an approximation of the whole matrix from that slice:

\[\mathbf{N} = \mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}^{\top}\]
Nyström approximation of the Hessian at the client. Only the sampled columns and the small intersection block are ever computed.

Keeping the direction usable. A deep model’s loss is non-convex, so its Hessian is indefinite and a direction built from it need not point downhill. The \(\ell_2\) regularizer already in the objective carries into the sampled blocks, making \(\mathbf{M}\) positive definite. The sketch is a fixed-rank Nyström approximation, the step is a descent direction, and the smallest eigenvalue is controlled, which is what the convergence argument needs.

Inverting without inverting. The Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity turns the inverse of a \(d \times d\) matrix with rank-\(m\) structure into the inverse of an \(m \times m\) matrix plus matrix-vector products. With \(m\) in the tens and \(d\) in the millions, that is the difference between impossible and routine.

Keeping curvature private. Sending a curvature estimate is not obviously privacy-preserving, so a one-pass ADMM step runs over the sketched Hessian, and what leaves the client is an ADMM-passed update:

\[\mathbf{y}_i^k = \big(\mathbf{N}^k_i + \alpha_k\mathbf{I} + \rho\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{g}_i^k - \lambda_i^{k-1} + \rho\,\mathbf{y}^{k-1}\big)\]

The server averages across clients, updates the global weights, and each client updates its dual variable. One pass, not an inner loop, which keeps client cost near first-order. Per global update this gives \(O(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})\) communication and \(O(d\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})\) convergence time.

Curvature is sketched on-device, passed through one ADMM step, and aggregated at the parameter server.

Results

Communication time per round, under matched model and hyperparameter settings:

Model DONE FedNew FedNS Nys-Fed
ResNet-18 (11.4M params) ≥ 48 h ≥ 132 h ≥ 48 h 130 to 141 s
ResNet-50 (23.9M params) ≥ 56 h ≥ 156 h ≥ 100 h 700 to 820 s
Custom DNN (0.1M params) ≥ 20 h ≥ 72 h ≥ 100 h 105 to 250 s

That is a change in what the hardware can do, not a tuning win.

One asymmetry worth stating, because it cuts against the easy reading: for FedNew and FedNS on the deep models, parameters were frozen up to the last layer and the inverse Hessian computed only for that layer. Nys-Fed sketched over all learnable parameters. The baselines were given the easier job and still took hours.

Evaluated on femnist and shakespeare from LEAF (the latter non-IID across 1,129 clients with a 1.1M-parameter LSTM), cinic10 under a Dirichlet split, and phishing / w8a / realsim from libsvm with logistic and L2-SVM objectives. Baselines span both families: FedAvg, FedProx, FedAdam on one side; FedNew, FedNS, DONE on the other.

Limitations

  • Communication time per round, not end-to-end training time. The tables measure the per-round cost and are not full training comparisons.
  • DONE ran with one Richardson iteration. Its own paper reports better convergence with more; this is the setting that matches the single-local-update framework, which is the fair framework comparison but not DONE at its best.
  • The sketch size is a hyperparameter. The convex tables vary it from 8 to 64 columns deliberately, and cost scales with it.
  • The gains depend on \(d\) being large. That is the premise: the sketch avoids a cost you only pay at scale.
  • Classification and language modelling only. No claim about other task families.
