Variance reduction with Barzilai-Borwein step sizes

SVRG removes most of the noise from stochastic gradients but still needs a step size. The Barzilai-Borwein rule derives one from gradients already computed, making it essentially free curvature information.

SVRG with Barzilai-Borwein curvature

My contribution. Method design, the convergence proof, implementation and experiments. With Nobuo Yamashita; published in the Journal of Industrial and Management Optimization.

The problem

Fitting a model to data means minimising an average loss over \(n\) training examples:

\[\min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^d} \; f(w) \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i(w)\]

Computing the full gradient \(\nabla f(w)\) costs a pass over all \(n\) examples. When \(n\) is large that is too slow, so stochastic gradient descent samples one example (or a small batch) and steps on that instead:

\[w_{t+1} \;=\; w_t - \eta_t \nabla f_{i_t}(w_t), \qquad i_t \sim \text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,n\}\]

The estimate is unbiased, so on average it points the right way. The difficulty is variance: each individual step can point almost anywhere, and the iterates fluctuate rather than descending. To converge at all, the step size has to shrink toward zero, which is what makes SGD slow in its final phase.

Variance reduction

SVRG removes most of that noise with a control variate. Periodically it computes a full gradient at a snapshot point \(\tilde{w}\) and stores it. Between snapshots the update is

\[v_t \;=\; \nabla f_{i_t}(w_t) \;-\; \nabla f_{i_t}(\tilde{w}) \;+\; \nabla f(\tilde{w})\]

This is still unbiased, since the two correction terms have equal expectation. But its variance shrinks as both \(w_t\) and \(\tilde w\) approach the solution, because the first two terms are then evaluated at nearby points and largely cancel. With the noise controlled, a constant step size becomes viable, and on strongly convex problems SVRG recovers linear convergence.

That leaves one question: what should the constant step size be? In practice it does most of the work, and in practice it is tuned by hand.

Barzilai-Borwein step sizes

The Barzilai-Borwein rule answers that from information already in hand. Write the differences between successive iterates and gradients:

\[s_t \;=\; w_t - w_{t-1}, \qquad y_t \;=\; \nabla f(w_t) - \nabla f(w_{t-1})\]

A quasi-Newton method looks for a matrix \(B\) satisfying the secant equation \(B s_t = y_t\). The Barzilai-Borwein idea is to insist that \(B\) be a multiple of the identity, \(B = \alpha^{-1} I\), and solve the resulting least-squares problem. That gives two step sizes:

\[\alpha_t^{\mathrm{BB1}} \;=\; \frac{s_t^{\top} s_t}{s_t^{\top} y_t}, \qquad \alpha_t^{\mathrm{BB2}} \;=\; \frac{s_t^{\top} y_t}{y_t^{\top} y_t}\]

Two things make this attractive. It is curvature information, since it is derived from the secant equation, so it adapts to the local geometry rather than following a fixed schedule. And it is free: \(s_t\) and \(y_t\) are built from quantities the algorithm already computed. No Hessian, no extra pass over the data, two inner products.

What we did

We incorporated Barzilai-Borwein curvature into SVRG, computing the step size at each snapshot from the full gradients that SVRG already evaluates there, and separately studied using the BB step size itself as a variant.

The part I would point to is the theorem, not the algorithm. We proved a linear convergence result that covers not only this method but other existing SVRG variants that use second-order information. Several such variants were already in use without a convergence guarantee of that generality. Closing that gap in the surrounding literature is a more useful contribution than one more algorithm, because it justifies methods other people were already running.

Experiments

Gisette at regularization $$10^{-5}$$. Left: optimality gap against CPU time. Right: the variance of the gradient estimate per epoch, which is the quantity the method is designed to control.

Two problem families were used, both convex:

L2-regularized logistic regression.

\[f(w) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log\!\big(1 + \exp(-y_i\, x_i^{\top} w)\big) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2\]

L2-regularized squared-hinge SVM.

\[f(w) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\!\big(0,\, 1 - y_i\, x_i^{\top} w\big)^{2} \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2\]

Datasets: gisette, covtype, ijcnn1 and MNIST, spanning dense and sparse regimes and a range of dimensions.

The proposed method with a constant step size outperforms existing variance-reduced methods on several of these problems. The right-hand figure above is the more informative one: it shows the variance of the gradient estimate falling, which is the mechanism rather than the outcome.

Limitations

  • Several, not all. It does not win everywhere, and the paper reports which problems it does not. A method paper that reports only its own wins is less useful than one that marks the boundary.
  • Barzilai-Borwein step sizes can oscillate. The rule is not monotone and can produce wildly varying step sizes on ill-conditioned problems. The stabilising safeguards are part of the method, not an implementation detail.
  • Convex objectives only. The convergence theorem does not cover non-convex losses, and I do not claim it does.
  • Full gradients at snapshots. SVRG’s periodic full-gradient computation is a real cost, and it is what makes the method less attractive in the very large \(n\) regime where even one pass is expensive.
