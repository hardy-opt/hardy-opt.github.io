Continuous and discrete optimization: a working map

Created on September 24, 2026

2026   ·   optimization   math   ·   optimization

Almost every optimization problem reduces to the same three objects: variables you can choose, an objective to make small, and constraints that say which choices are allowed.

\[\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}} \; f(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0, \quad h_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0\]

What determines whether the problem takes a second or a century is the structure: whether \(\mathcal{X}\) is continuous or discrete, whether \(f\) is convex, whether you can compute derivatives, and whether the data is known or random.

The single biggest fork is continuous versus discrete, and the practical difference is larger than the notation suggests. This post is the map I wish I had been given early: what lives on each side, which methods belong where, how to diagnose which kind of problem you have, and what happens at the border: which is where most interesting applied work actually sits.

Part I · Continuous optimization

Variables are real-valued. You can take derivatives, make arbitrarily small steps, and ask what happens in a neighbourhood. The local geometry is informative, which is what makes the whole theory possible.

The dividing line: convexity

A convex problem has a convex objective over a convex feasible set. Its consequence is the most useful single fact in optimization:

Every local minimum is global, and there is a certificate that you have found it.

So “did I find the answer?” has a yes-or-no answer. For convex problems with structure we have polynomial-time algorithms, mature solvers, and duality theory that turns the question “is this optimal?” into an arithmetic check. Convex optimization is, for practical purposes, a solved technology.

Non-convex is everything else, and the honest summary is that you are looking for a good point rather than the point. What we have are methods that reliably find stationary points, plus empirical knowledge about which landscapes are forgiving. Deep learning is the famous case where non-convexity turned out to matter far less than everyone feared.

The conic hierarchy

Convex problems come in a nested family, and knowing where yours sits tells you which solver to use:

Class Objective / constraints Example
LP linear / linear production planning, network flow, diet problem
QP quadratic / linear portfolio variance, SVM dual, MPC
SOCP linear / second-order cone robust LP, facility location with distances
SDP linear / positive-semidefinite relaxations of hard combinatorial problems, control

Each contains the one above it. The interesting fact is that interior-point methods solve all of them with essentially one algorithmic idea (follow a central path toward the boundary) which is why a single solver can handle the whole hierarchy.

Unconstrained methods, and what each one buys

Everything here is a different answer to: how much do you know about the local shape?

Gradient descent, \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)\). Uses the slope only. Cheap, and slow when the problem is badly conditioned: the convergence rate degrades with the condition number, which is why a long narrow valley makes it zig-zag.

Momentum / Nesterov acceleration, accumulate past directions. For convex problems this improves the rate from \(O(1/k)\) to \(O(1/k^2)\), and that is provably the best a first-order method can do. Acceleration is not a trick; it is a matching upper bound.

Newton’s method, \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f\). Uses curvature. Quadratic local convergence; condition number stops mattering because you are rescaling by the geometry itself. Costs \(O(d^3)\) per step, and on a non-convex problem \(\mathbf{H}\) can be indefinite, in which case the “descent” direction goes uphill.

Quasi-Newton (BFGS, L-BFGS), build a curvature estimate from successive gradients via the secant equation. The practical sweet spot for medium-scale smooth problems, and L-BFGS’s limited memory makes it workable at high dimension. Its weakness: the estimate is built from gradient differences, so with stochastic gradients you are differencing noise.

Trust-region and line-search methods, two ways to answer “how far should I step?”. A line search picks the step length along a fixed direction; a trust region fixes a radius you trust the model within and optimises inside it. Trust regions are more robust on non-convex problems, because they handle indefinite curvature gracefully instead of needing a fix.

Sketched and randomized second-order methods, compute curvature on a random subspace. Sample \(m \ll d\) Hessian columns and reconstruct via the Nyström method, bringing cost from \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\) to \(O(ndm + dm^2)\). This is my own area; the design problem is getting a usable descent direction out of a low-rank approximation of a possibly indefinite matrix, which regularisation solves.

Derivative-free methods (Nelder-Mead, pattern search, CMA-ES): when there is no gradient: simulation output, a physical experiment, a black box. Slow and sometimes the only option.

Bayesian optimization: when evaluations are expensive as well as derivative-free. Build a probabilistic surrogate, use an acquisition function to choose the next point. Dozens of evaluations, not thousands.

Constrained methods

KKT conditions generalise “the gradient is zero” to constrained problems: at an optimum, the objective gradient is a combination of active constraint gradients. The multipliers are shadow prices (how much the optimum would improve if a constraint were relaxed) which is often more useful to a practitioner than the solution itself.

Lagrangian duality turns the constrained problem into an unconstrained one in the multipliers. The dual is always a lower bound; for convex problems the gap is zero. That gap is what lets you certify optimality.

The main families:

  • Interior-point, add a barrier that blows up at the boundary, shrink it. Workhorse for convex problems at scale.
  • Active-set, guess which constraints bind, solve the equality-constrained problem, repeat.
  • Augmented Lagrangian / ADMM, penalise violation and update multipliers. ADMM splits a problem into pieces that are each easy, and is the reason so much distributed and federated optimization is built on it: the splitting maps onto the machines.
  • Projected and proximal gradient, step, then project back into the feasible set. The proximal version handles non-smooth terms, which is what makes \(\ell_1\) regularisation tractable and gives you sparsity.

Stochastic optimization

When the objective is an expectation, over data, or over genuine randomness:

\[\min_{\mathbf{x}} \; \mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[F(\mathbf{x},\xi)\right]\]

SGD replaces the gradient with a cheap unbiased estimate. The variance of that estimate, not the curvature, sets the convergence rate: which is a different world from the deterministic case and calls for different methods.

Variance reduction (SVRG, SAGA, SARAH) uses a control variate, typically a periodically refreshed full gradient, to cut the noise, recovering linear convergence on strongly convex problems. My JIMO paper sits here: using Barzilai-Borwein step sizes as cheap second-order information inside SVRG, where “cheap” means the gradients were already computed.

Robust and chance-constrained optimization handle uncertainty differently, optimise the worst case over an uncertainty set, or require constraints to hold with probability \(1-\epsilon\). Often the right framing in engineering, where a constraint violation is a failure rather than a cost.

Part II · Discrete optimization

Variables take values in a finite or countable set: integers, binary choices, permutations, subsets, graph structures. Scheduling, routing, assignment, network design, and most of operations research.

Why it is categorically harder

There is no local geometry. You cannot take a derivative of a decision. You cannot take a small step, flipping one binary variable may be the only move available, and it may be a large change. A solution can be optimal among all its neighbours and far from the global optimum, with nothing local to indicate it.

And the problems are genuinely hard: TSP, knapsack, set cover, graph colouring are NP-hard, so barring a complexity surprise there is no polynomial algorithm. This is not a gap in our knowledge of algorithms; it is believed to be a property of the problems.

So the discipline changes. In continuous convex optimization you ask “what is the optimum?”. In discrete optimization you ask one of:

  • What is the best solution I can find in the time available? (heuristics)
  • How far from optimal might I be? (bounds and gaps)
  • Can I prove optimality for instances of this size? (exact methods)

Exact methods

Branch and bound is the core idea and it is beautiful in its simplicity. Relax the integrality constraint and solve the easy continuous problem. That gives a bound. If the relaxed solution happens to be integral, you are done. Otherwise pick a fractional variable and branch: one subproblem with \(x \le \lfloor v \rfloor\), one with \(x \ge \lceil v \rceil\). Recurse. Any subtree whose bound is worse than the best solution found is pruned without being explored.

The whole art is making the bound tight, because a tight bound prunes aggressively and a loose one makes you enumerate.

Cutting planes tighten the relaxation by adding valid inequalities that cut off fractional solutions without removing any integer point. Branch and cut (doing both) is what every modern MILP solver actually is, and why Gurobi and CPLEX routinely solve problems with hundreds of thousands of integer variables that are “NP-hard” in the textbook sense. The theory says worst case; the solvers exploit structure.

Dynamic programming works when the problem has optimal substructure: shortest paths, knapsack, sequence alignment. Exact and polynomial in the state space, and the state space is usually what explodes.

Constraint programming takes a different route: propagate constraints to shrink variable domains, search what remains. Often dominant on scheduling and rostering, where the constraints are complex and combinatorial rather than numerical.

SAT and SMT solvers reduce the problem to Boolean satisfiability. Modern CDCL solvers handle millions of clauses, which is one of the genuinely surprising engineering achievements in computing.

When exact is out of reach

Approximation algorithms come with a proven guarantee, within a factor of optimal, always. The greedy algorithm for set cover is within \(\ln n\); for submodular maximisation under a cardinality constraint, greedy achieves \(1 - 1/e\) and that is provably the best possible. A guarantee is worth a great deal in a setting where you otherwise have no idea how good your answer is.

Metaheuristics have no guarantee and often work well: simulated annealing (accept worse solutions with decreasing probability, borrowed from statistical physics), tabu search (forbid recent moves to escape local optima), genetic and evolutionary algorithms, ant colony optimization, large neighbourhood search. The last is the most practically effective for routing: destroy part of a solution and rebuild it optimally.

Heuristic search, A*, best-first, beam search, Monte Carlo Tree Search. Here the heuristic function is everything: a good admissible heuristic can turn an intractable search into a quick one, and the whole engineering problem becomes estimating cost-to-go well. I work on exactly this in my current role, in a combinatorial AND/OR search setting.

Part III · Where the two halves meet

Most interesting applied problems live here, and this is the part that pure-continuous or pure-discrete training leaves out.

Relaxation. Drop integrality, solve the continuous problem, and you get a bound plus a starting point. This is the engine inside branch and bound, and it is why discrete optimization depends so heavily on continuous optimization being fast.

LP relaxation and rounding. Solve the LP, round the fractional solution, prove the rounding costs you at most a factor. Randomised rounding gives some of the best known approximation guarantees.

Semidefinite relaxation. The Goemans-Williamson algorithm for MAX-CUT relaxes a binary quadratic problem to an SDP and rounds with a random hyperplane, achieving 0.878 of optimal. A continuous, convex relaxation of an NP-hard discrete problem, with a proven guarantee, one of the most elegant results in the field.

MINLP. Both continuous and discrete variables with nonlinear functions: chemical process design, unit commitment in power systems, portfolio problems with transaction lots. Genuinely hard, and exactly what industrial problems look like.

Decomposition. Benders splits a problem into a master problem over the discrete variables and subproblems over the continuous ones, iterating by passing cuts. Dantzig-Wolfe and column generation do the dual thing (generate variables on demand) and are how vehicle routing and crew scheduling are solved at airline scale.

Submodularity is the closest discrete analogue of convexity. Diminishing returns on sets gives you provable guarantees for greedy algorithms, and it shows up in sensor placement, feature selection, summarisation and data selection.

Optimal transport sits precisely on the boundary. The discrete problem is an assignment problem a linear program with integral optimal solutions by total unimodularity. The entropy-regularised continuous version is solved by Sinkhorn iterations in a few lines, which is why OT became practical in machine learning.

How to tell which problem you have

A decision procedure I actually use:

1 · Are the decisions divisible? “How much” is continuous; “which one”, “how many”, “yes or no” is discrete. Mixing both means MINLP, and you should expect difficulty.

2 · Can you compute a gradient? Analytically or by automatic differentiation, use a first- or second-order method. If not, is each evaluation cheap (derivative-free) or expensive (Bayesian optimization)?

3 · Is it convex? Check the composition rules rather than guessing: affine functions, norms, max of convex functions, and compositions respecting curvature and monotonicity. If it is convex, say so and use the right solver, you get a certificate of optimality, which is worth a lot.

4 · How large, and in what dimension? \(10^3\) variables and \(10^9\) variables are different engineering problems with the same mathematics. For discrete problems, ask instead how large the relaxation gap is, that, not the variable count, predicts whether branch and bound will finish.

5 · Is the data known? Deterministic, stochastic (optimise an expectation), or robust (optimise the worst case) are three different problems with the same description in words, and choosing the wrong one is a common and expensive mistake.

6 · What resource is actually scarce? Time, memory, communication rounds, instrument hours, money. This determines what you are really minimising, and it is the question most often skipped including in the academic literature, which tends to count iterations because iterations are easy to count.

Why both halves are worth knowing

I was trained in continuous optimization, my thesis was second-order methods for large-scale unconstrained problems, which is about as continuous as it gets. Industrial problems have not respected the boundary once.

A real problem arrives as “schedule these experiments on this instrument within this budget”, and it contains a continuous surrogate model, a discrete selection, and a resource constraint, all at once. The continuous theory tells you how to fit the surrogate and how fast you can descend. The discrete theory tells you that selection is a search and that a good bound beats a clever heuristic. The boundary techniques (relaxation, decomposition, submodularity) are how the two get connected.

Knowing only one half means reaching for the wrong tool roughly half the time, and the failure is quiet: you get an answer, it is plausible, and you never learn it was the wrong formulation.
