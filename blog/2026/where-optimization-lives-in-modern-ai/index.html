Where optimization actually lives in modern AI

Created on October 17, 2026

2026   ·   optimization   MLDL   ·   optimization

“Machine learning is just optimization” is true in the way that “chemistry is just physics” is true: accurate, and not useful on its own. The useful version is more specific, modern AI contains many distinct optimization problems, with different structure, different constraints, and different right answers. Training is one of them. It is not the hardest one, and it is no longer the most expensive one.

This is a map of them. For each: what is being minimised, what actually solves it, why that method and not another, and where the open problems are. I have tried to be honest about which parts are well understood and which are still folklore.

1. The one everyone means: empirical risk minimisation

Fit parameters \(\mathbf{w}\) to data by minimising average loss:

\[\min_{\mathbf{w}} \; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\!\left(f(\mathbf{x}_i;\mathbf{w}),\, y_i\right) + \lambda R(\mathbf{w})\]

That is it. Logistic regression, a ResNet and a 70-billion-parameter language model are all this problem; they differ in whether \(f\) is linear, how big \(n\) and \(d\) are, and whether the objective is convex.

Convex case (\(f\) linear, \(\ell\) convex): solved, in the strong sense that we have methods with proofs, rates matching lower bounds, and reliable software. Any local minimum is global.

Non-convex case (anything deep): not solved, and the interesting discovery of the last decade is that it mostly does not matter. In high dimension, local minima are rarely the obstacle, saddle points and ill-conditioning are. Most minima found by SGD generalise comparably. The practical question shifted from “will we find a good minimum?” to “how cheaply, and which one?”

What is used, and why

SGD with momentum. Use a mini-batch estimate of the gradient. Momentum accumulates an exponentially weighted average of past gradients, which damps oscillation across a narrow valley and accelerates along it. Still the best choice for convolutional vision models, and still competitive when well tuned.

Adam and AdamW. Per-parameter adaptive step sizes from running estimates of the gradient’s first and second moments. This is a diagonal preconditioner: it rescales each coordinate by its own recent gradient magnitude. It is a crude curvature approximation, and the crudeness is the point \(O(d)\) memory and arithmetic, no matrices.

AdamW’s decoupling of weight decay from the adaptive update is a small change that matters disproportionately, because with Adam the \(\ell_2\) penalty gets divided by the same adaptive denominator as the gradient and stops acting like weight decay at all.

Why Adam dominates transformers is still partly empirical. The most credible explanation is that transformer loss landscapes are badly conditioned across coordinate blocks. Embeddings, attention projections and layer-norm parameters have gradient scales orders of magnitude apart, and diagonal rescaling handles exactly that. SGD has to pick one learning rate for all of them.

Learning-rate schedules are part of the optimizer, not a detail. Warmup, cosine decay, and the various cyclical schemes change which minimum you land in, not just how fast you get there. Warmup in particular exists because adaptive methods have unreliable second-moment estimates in the first few hundred steps, and a large step taken on a bad estimate is hard to recover from.

Second-order methods: the standing puzzle

Newton’s method converges quadratically near a solution. For \(d\) parameters, forming and inverting the Hessian is \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\). At \(d = 10^9\) this is not a large constant factor, it is physically impossible.

So the field approximates:

  • L-BFGS builds an implicit inverse-Hessian estimate from a short history of gradient differences. Excellent for deterministic, medium-scale problems; it struggles with stochastic gradients, because the curvature estimate is built from differences that are now mostly noise.
  • K-FAC approximates the Fisher information as a Kronecker product of two small factors per layer. Tractable, and genuinely used.
  • Shampoo maintains full-matrix preconditioners per tensor dimension, and in distributed form is now used in production training.
  • Sophia, Muon and the recent wave use cheap diagonal or orthogonalised curvature estimates with clipping, aiming for second-order benefit at near-first-order cost.
  • Sketching and low-rank methods compute curvature on a random subspace. Sample \(m \ll d\) Hessian columns and reconstruct via the Nyström method: cost drops to \(O(ndm + dm^2)\). This is where my own work sits, including the federated version where the binding constraint is a client device’s memory rather than a datacentre’s.

My honest read: second-order methods are undervalued in the well-conditioned, abundant-data regime and genuinely valuable when data is scarce, the problem is ill-conditioned, or each gradient is expensive. Pretraining is the first regime. Fine-tuning, scientific ML and federated learning are the second. The field optimised hard for the first and under-invested in the second.

2. Training at scale is a different problem

Once a model does not fit on one device, the optimization problem acquires a systems constraint, and the constraint starts dictating the mathematics.

Optimizer state is the memory problem, not the weights. Adam stores two moment estimates per parameter. In mixed precision with master weights you are at roughly 16 bytes per parameter: a 7B model needs about 112 GB before activations. ZeRO and FSDP shard optimizer state, gradients and parameters across devices, trading communication for memory. That is a constrained optimization problem about the training system, solved by engineers, and it determines what is trainable.

Gradient accumulation and large-batch training change the statistics. Beyond a critical batch size, more samples per step stop buying proportionally better gradient estimates, and the right response is a learning-rate rule (linear scaling, LARS/LAMB layer-wise adaptation) derived from that noise structure.

Communication-efficient distributed optimization, gradient compression, quantised updates, error feedback, local SGD, is a well-developed theory about how much you can corrupt a gradient before convergence degrades. Federated learning is the extreme case: data cannot move at all, so communication rounds become the scarce resource.

3. Alignment is constrained optimization

This is where the problem structure changes most, and it is the part people find most surprising.

RLHF. You want a policy \(\pi\) that scores well under a learned reward \(r\) without drifting far from the reference model:

\[\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[r(x,y)\right] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\right)\]

That is a KL-regularised policy optimization problem. PPO solves it with a clipped surrogate objective and a trust-region flavour, for a reason familiar from classical optimization: a step that is too large in policy space produces a catastrophically wrong policy, so you constrain step size in a distributional metric rather than in parameter space.

DPO is the part a optimization person should find elegant. The constrained problem above has a closed-form optimal policy in terms of the reward. Invert that relation and the reward model disappears; you are left with a simple classification loss on preference pairs. No reward model, no RL loop, no rollouts. It is the same optimization problem, re-parameterised until it becomes convex-ish supervised learning.

There is a real lesson there. A substantial fraction of progress in “AI methods” is reformulation finding a change of variables under which a hard problem becomes an easy one. That is what optimization training teaches you to look for, and it transfers.

GRPO and the reasoning-model wave replace the learned reward with a verifiable one (did the program run? is the proof valid?) and estimate advantages from groups of sampled completions. The optimization structure is similar; the reward being verifiable changes the variance properties substantially.

Constrained alignment. Multiple objectives (be helpful, be harmless, be honest) is multi-objective optimization, usually handled as a Lagrangian with learned multipliers. The theory here is classical and the practice is not yet mature.

4. Generative models: the objective is the design choice

Diffusion models are trained with a denoising objective, predict the noise added at a random timestep: which is a tractable surrogate for an intractable likelihood. The training problem is plain supervised regression and well behaved.

The interesting optimization is at sampling time. Generation means integrating a reverse-time SDE, or its deterministic probability-flow ODE, from noise to data. That is numerical analysis, not learning: DDIM, DPM-Solver and the exponential-integrator family are ODE solvers exploiting the semi-linear structure of the flow. Going from 1,000 sampling steps to 20 was a numerical-methods advance, not a modelling one.

Then there is distillation (consistency models, progressive distillation) which pose the question “can a one-step map approximate the solution operator of this ODE?” as an optimization problem in its own right.

Flow matching reframes generation as learning a velocity field transporting one distribution to another, which puts optimal transport directly into the objective. Straighter transport paths mean fewer integration steps, so the optimization and the sampling cost are coupled by design.

Normalising flows and VAEs trade expressiveness against tractability of the likelihood: a constrained design problem, where the constraint is “the Jacobian determinant must be computable”.

5. Inference-time optimization: now the expensive one

A frontier model is trained once and served billions of times. The economics moved, and so did the interesting problems.

Quantisation. Represent weights and activations in 4 or 8 bits with minimal quality loss. This is genuinely a constrained optimization problem: minimise output deviation subject to a bit budget. GPTQ solves a layer-wise reconstruction problem using second-order information, approximate Hessians of the layer output with respect to weights, which is exactly the curvature machinery from §1 applied to compression. AWQ exploits the empirical fact that a small fraction of channels matter disproportionately, which is a sparsity structure in the solution.

Pruning. Which weights can be removed? Classical formulations (Optimal Brain Surgeon) use the Hessian of the loss to estimate the damage from removing each weight; modern structured pruning removes whole heads or channels under a hardware-shaped constraint, because unstructured sparsity does not speed anything up on a GPU.

Speculative decoding. A small draft model proposes tokens, the large model verifies in parallel. Choosing draft length is a sequential decision problem under uncertainty, accept-rate against verification cost, and the right draft length is not constant across a sequence.

KV-cache management, continuous batching, paged attention. Scheduling and allocation problems: which requests to batch, which cache blocks to evict, how to page attention memory. Classic combinatorial optimization, worth enormous amounts of money, and mostly solved by good heuristics rather than by anything principled.

Mixture-of-experts routing. Assign tokens to experts subject to capacity constraints: a balanced assignment problem. Some implementations use Sinkhorn iterations, i.e. entropy-regularised optimal transport. Most use a cheap top-\(k\) with an auxiliary load-balancing loss, which is the constraint relaxed into a penalty because the exact version is too slow.

6. Discrete and combinatorial optimization inside the models

Decoding is search. Finding the most likely sequence under an autoregressive model is a combinatorial problem over an exponentially large tree. Greedy decoding and beam search are heuristics; sampling is a deliberate choice not to optimise. Constrained decoding, enforce this grammar, match this JSON schema, is combinatorial search under constraints, solved with masks and automata.

Tree search for reasoning. Monte Carlo Tree Search over reasoning steps, with the model as both policy and value function, is AlphaZero’s machinery applied to text. The exploration-exploitation problem is the same, and UCT and PUCT are the same bandit algorithms.

Attention sparsity patterns, architecture search, operator scheduling. Which tokens attend to which; which operators to fuse; how to tile a matrix multiplication for a particular memory hierarchy. The last one is an integer program that compilers solve with heuristics, and it is where a large fraction of real-world speedup comes from.

7. Kernel methods, and why they are not a historical footnote

Kernel machines were displaced by deep learning, and the optimization problems they pose are still worth knowing, partly because they keep reappearing inside modern systems.

The core problem is convex, and that is the point. Training a support vector machine means solving a quadratic program with box constraints:

\[\max_{\alpha} \; \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j K(x_i, x_j) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C, \; \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\]

Any local optimum is global and there is a duality certificate, so unlike a neural network you can know you are done. Sequential minimal optimization solves it by repeatedly picking two coordinates and optimising them analytically, which is a coordinate-descent idea that long predates its rediscovery in large-scale machine learning.

The scaling obstacle is the kernel matrix. \(K\) is \(n \times n\). At a million samples that is \(10^{12}\) entries, and this is exactly the arithmetic that makes Hessians intractable, one level across. The standard escape is the same one: low-rank approximation. The Nyström method samples \(m \ll n\) columns and reconstructs \(K \approx C M^{-1} C^{\top}\); random Fourier features instead approximate the kernel by an explicit randomised feature map. Both convert an intractable dense problem into a tractable low-rank one, and the quality-versus-rank trade-off is the whole engineering question.

This is the same construction I use on the Hessian rather than on a Gram matrix. The mathematics transfers directly, which is the useful observation: sampling a matrix you cannot afford to build is one technique with many addresses.

Where kernels persist in modern systems. Gaussian processes are kernel methods, so every Bayesian optimization loop is solving a kernel problem, and the \(O(n^3)\) cost of the posterior is why inducing-point and sparse approximations exist. Attention is a kernel-like similarity computation, and a visible line of efficient-transformer work (Performer and its relatives) applies random-feature kernel approximation directly to it. Kernel methods also remain the right choice when data is scarce, which is common in scientific applications.

8. Multi-objective optimization: when there is no single best answer

Most of this post assumes one scalar to minimise. A great many real problems have several, and they disagree.

A drug candidate should be potent, soluble, non-toxic and synthesisable. A model should be accurate, small, fast and fair. An experiment should be informative and cheap. These trade off, and no ordering of them is objectively correct.

The honest formulation. With objectives \(f_1,\dots,f_k\), a point \(x\) dominates \(x'\) if it is no worse on every objective and strictly better on at least one. The solutions that nothing dominates form the Pareto front, and the goal is to characterise that front rather than to return one point.

\[\mathcal{P} = \{\, x \in \mathcal{X} \;:\; \nexists\, x' \in \mathcal{X} \text{ with } x' \succ x \,\}\]

The three practical approaches.

Scalarise. Collapse to one objective with weights, \(\sum_i w_i f_i(x)\), and solve repeatedly with different weights to trace the front. Simple, and it has a real failure mode: a weighted sum cannot reach points on a non-convex region of the front, no matter what weights you choose. Chebyshev scalarisation fixes that, which is why it is preferred in serious work.

Constrain. Optimise one objective and impose bounds on the rest, the \(\epsilon\)-constraint method. Often the most natural framing in engineering, because the constraints are usually real requirements rather than preferences.

Attack the front directly. Evolutionary methods (NSGA-II and successors) maintain a population approximating the whole front. In the expensive-evaluation regime, multi-objective Bayesian optimization uses acquisition functions over the front itself: expected hypervolume improvement asks how much a candidate would grow the volume dominated by the current front.

Why it matters for AI specifically. Alignment is multi-objective, and the field mostly handles it by scalarising with a KL penalty, which is one point on a front nobody has characterised. Model compression is multi-objective: accuracy against latency against memory. Neural architecture search is routinely posed over accuracy and FLOPs together. In each case the Pareto structure is the thing you would actually want to see, and in each case the practice is to pick a weighting and report a single number.

9. Optimization of the pipeline

Hyperparameter optimization. Each evaluation costs a training run, so you have an expensive black-box problem: the native setting for Bayesian optimization: fit a surrogate (usually a Gaussian process or a tree ensemble), use an acquisition function to decide where to look next. Hyperband and ASHA add early stopping, which turns it into a bandit problem with partial information.

A detail I care about, having worked on it: the literature mostly counts iterations, while real campaigns are limited by spend, and different configurations cost different amounts. Counting the wrong resource changes which method looks best. I wrote about that here.

Data selection and curriculum. Which examples to train on, in what order, is a combinatorial problem over subsets with a submodular flavour, and at frontier scale it is one of the highest- leverage decisions available, because it changes the quality of every subsequent step.

Neural architecture search. Discrete search over architectures, usually relaxed to continuous (DARTS) to make gradients available: the standard convex-relaxation move from classical discrete optimization, applied to architecture.

10. What is well solved, and what is not

Well solved

  • Convex problems, at essentially any scale that matters
  • First-order stochastic optimization for over-parameterised models
  • Distributed data-parallel training, as engineering
  • Layer-wise post-training quantisation

Not solved

  • Second-order methods at scale. Everything used is a diagonal or block approximation. The gap between what curvature could give and what we can afford is still wide, and it is where I think the most under-exploited leverage sits.
  • Non-convex theory that predicts practice. We have rates under assumptions nobody verifies. The reason Adam beats SGD on transformers is not settled.
  • Data selection, in any principled sense.
  • Inference scheduling, enormous economic value, almost entirely heuristic.
  • Multi-objective alignment, where the Pareto structure is barely characterised (see section 8).
  • Non-myopic sequential decision-making under realistic budgets: the theory is clear and the practice usually does not pay, for reasons that are not well understood.

Why this map is worth having

Two reasons, and the second is the one I would argue for.

The first is practical: when you can see which of these problems you are actually facing, you know which literature to read. A badly-conditioned fine-tune, a sampling bottleneck and a serving scheduler are three different problems, and recognising which one you have saves weeks.

The second is that the structure transfers and the application does not. KL-constrained policy optimization is a trust region. Quantisation is constrained approximation with second-order error estimates. MoE routing is balanced assignment. Diffusion sampling is ODE integration. Decoding is tree search. Someone who knows the structure can walk into a new application and recognise the problem: which is, in the end, what a mathematical-optimization training is for.
