What a Hessian sketch actually buys you

Created on April 20, 2025

2025   ·   optimization   second-order   ·   optimization

Every introduction to optimization tells you the same two things. Gradient descent is cheap and slow. Newton’s method is expensive and fast. Then almost every practitioner uses the cheap one and spends the savings on hyperparameter tuning.

That is the right call more often than the textbooks imply. But it is worth understanding exactly what is expensive, because the expensive part is narrower than people assume, and once you know which term dominates, you can go after it.

The arithmetic

Suppose you are minimising a loss over \(d\) parameters using \(n\) data points.

Gradient descent needs the gradient: one pass over the data, \(O(nd)\) per step. It tells you which direction is downhill.

Newton’s method also wants the Hessian: the \(d \times d\) matrix of second derivatives, which tells you how the slope is changing. Forming it costs \(O(nd^2)\), and solving the linear system \(H \delta w = -g\) costs \(O(d^3)\).

That \(d^3\) is the problem. At \(d = 1{,}000\) it is a billion operations, annoying. At \(d = 10{,}000\) it is \(10^{12}\): a non-starter, and \(10{,}000\) parameters is a small model by any modern standard.

So the field went sideways instead. L-BFGS keeps a short history of gradient differences and builds an implicit curvature estimate from them, never forming a matrix at all. It works remarkably well, and it is still the default when someone needs a second-order method at scale.

The question worth asking

L-BFGS infers curvature from first-order information, from how the gradient has changed over the last few steps. That is clever, and it is also indirect: the secant equation it relies on becomes a poor proxy for the Newton step precisely when the problem is badly conditioned, which is when you wanted curvature in the first place.

So: is there a way to use the actual Hessian without paying for all of it?

Sketching

Here is the idea. Do not compute the whole Hessian. Sample \(m\) of its columns, where \(m \ll d\), say 10% of them. Now you have a \(d \times m\) slice of the matrix.

A \(d \times m\) slice is not a \(d \times d\) matrix, but the Nyström method lets you reconstruct an approximation of the whole thing from it. The construction is old. It comes from numerical integration, and it is the same machinery behind low-rank kernel approximation. The cost comes out as

\[O(ndm + dm^2)\]

instead of \(O(nd^2 + d^3)\). With \(m = 0.1d\), the \(d^3\) term becomes \(0.01 d^3\), and the \(nd^2\) term becomes \(0.1 nd^2\). Not a constant-factor win: an order-of-magnitude one, and it gets better as \(d\) grows.

In the experiments in our papers, \(m\) between 5% and 20% of \(d\) was enough to get most of the benefit. You can also skip forming the approximation entirely and compute the update \(\delta w\) directly, so neither the full Hessian nor its inverse is ever stored.

The part that nearly kills it

Everything above works on convex problems. Then you try it on a neural network.

On a non-convex objective the Hessian is indefinite: it has negative eigenvalues, because you are sitting near a saddle rather than a bowl. A direction built from an indefinite matrix is not guaranteed to point downhill at all. Your optimizer confidently takes a step that increases the loss.

Worse, the low-rank approximation does not save you. It inherits the problem: approximate an indefinite matrix and you get an indefinite approximation.

The fix is unglamorous and it is the reason the method works: regularize the sketch. Add a multiple of the identity, chosen so the result is positive definite. Now the direction is a descent direction by construction, you get a usable step, and you can prove global convergence.

That is the whole contribution, really. The Nyström part is classical. Making it survive contact with a non-convex loss is the part that took the work.

When not to do this

I want to be straightforward about this, because optimization papers are not always.

  • If \(d\) is small, use Newton. The sketch is machinery for avoiding a cost you are not paying.
  • If your gradients are very noisy (tiny batches, high-variance estimator) curvature estimated from that noise is worse than no curvature. A well-tuned first-order method wins, and it is simpler.
  • If your problem is well conditioned, gradient descent is already close to optimal and the extra complexity earns nothing.
  • You have to choose \(m\). It is a real hyperparameter, the method is sensitive to it, and the right value is problem-dependent. You can study the trade-off, approximation error against \(m\), convergence against \(m\), and we did, but the study informs the choice rather than removing it.

The honest summary: this is a method for large \(d\), ill-conditioned problems where you can afford a full-batch or large-batch gradient. That is a real and recurring setting, and it is not every setting.

Why I still think the direction matters

The ratio between what we spend on gradients and what we learn from them is bad, and it is getting worse as models grow. Every result that makes curvature information cheaper moves that ratio. Not all of them will matter; a sketch that works at \(d = 10^4\) is not automatically a sketch that works at \(d = 10^{10}\), and I would not claim otherwise without running it.

But the arithmetic at the top of this post is not going away, and \(d^3\) is a term worth attacking.
